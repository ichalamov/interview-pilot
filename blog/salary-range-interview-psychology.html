What Your Salary Range Really Says to Hiring Managers

Most candidates think a range is just a floor and a ceiling. Experienced hiring managers see something entirely different — and that gap in understanding is costing people real money.

InterviewAce
June 16, 2026
5 min read

Picture this: a senior-level candidate sits across from a hiring manager and says, "I'm looking for somewhere in the $180,000 to $210,000 range." They think they've just drawn two lines — a minimum they'll accept and a maximum they're hoping for.

The hiring manager hears something completely different.

This gap between what candidates think they're communicating and what experienced hiring managers actually hear is one of the most expensive misunderstandings in the modern job search. Here's how to close it.

The Two Numbers Hiring Managers Actually See

When you give a salary range in an interview, you're not describing a negotiation zone. You're revealing two very specific things about yourself — and every seasoned hiring manager knows exactly what to look for.

How a $180k–$210k range is really decoded
$180k
Your walk-away line
$210k
Your excitement number

The Bottom Number: Your Walk-Away Line

The lower end of your range is not "the minimum I'd accept if things go badly." It's interpreted as: offer me this and I'll probably say yes — but I'll keep one eye on the market from day one.

A candidate who accepts at their floor is still a candidate. They showed up. They took the role. But their enthusiasm is already discounted before they've signed the contract. They're not disengaged — not yet — but they're not bought in either.

The Top Number: Your Excitement Number

The upper end of your range is where the real signal lives. This is the number that makes counter-offers bounce off. It's the figure where a candidate walks in on day one already energised, already committed, already thinking about the long game instead of what's sitting in their inbox from other recruiters.

The insight: Everything in between those two numbers will likely result in a "yes" — but enthusiasm scales with where you land. The closer the offer is to the top, the more bought-in the person will be.

The $30,000 Question Hiring Managers Are Already Asking

Here's what makes the best hiring managers different: they're not asking "what's the least this person will take?"

They're asking "what number makes this person stop looking?"

Because they've done the maths. The gap between a walk-away offer and an excitement offer — often $20,000 to $30,000 at a senior level — is nothing compared to the real costs of getting it wrong:

  • 1

    Counter-offer panic. A candidate who accepted at their floor is exactly who a competitor will call six months later with a bump. Now you're in a reactive bidding war or you lose them anyway.

  • 2

    The month-9 resignation. Not angry, not dramatic — just a quiet departure to somewhere that valued them more from the start. Onboarding, productivity ramp-up, team disruption: all gone.

  • 3

    Re-running the search. Agency fees, recruiter time, interview rounds, reference checks. The cost of replacing a senior hire often runs 50–150% of their annual salary.

When you lay it out that way, an extra $30,000 on a senior hire isn't a cost — it's retention insurance.

Smart hiring managers know this. They're not adversaries in a negotiation — they're trying to figure out what number keeps you off the market. Give them the information they need to land in the right place.

What This Means for Your Next Interview

Understanding how your range is decoded changes how you should set it. Here's how to think about it going into your next salary conversation:

Set Your Floor With Intention

Your bottom number shouldn't be the minimum you could survive on. It should be the number you'd genuinely feel good accepting — a number that wouldn't leave you quietly bitter or eyeing other offers. If you'd resent taking a role at that salary, it's not actually your floor. Raise it.

Make Your Top Number Real

The top of your range should be defensible — rooted in market data, your experience level, and comparable roles. If you have the receipts (recent offer letters, compensation benchmarks, levelling data), you don't need to be vague. Specificity signals confidence and preparation. A candidate who says "I'm targeting $210,000 based on what I'm seeing at mid-to-late-stage fintechs at this level" is a fundamentally different conversation than one who throws out a number and hopes.

Give Them the Information They Need to Land Right

A salary range isn't a negotiation document. It's a map. When a smart hiring manager reads it correctly, they're using it to work out how to make you genuinely committed to this role — not just how to get you to sign.

If you share the range clearly and honestly, the best hiring managers will use it to make the right call. Read it that way, and both sides win.

The One Thing Most Candidates Get Wrong

Most candidates treat the salary conversation as a game — one where you hold back information to avoid anchoring too low or tipping your hand. That instinct makes sense, but it often backfires.

When you're vague or evasive about your salary expectations, hiring managers don't assume you're being strategic. They assume you haven't done your research, that you're desperate, or that you'll be difficult to work with later when it comes to an offer.

The candidates who handle salary conversations best come in prepared — knowing their number, knowing why it's right, and being clear about it. They don't hedge. They don't give a range so wide it means nothing. They say what they need and let the right role come to them.

That's not confidence you fake. It's confidence you prepare.
